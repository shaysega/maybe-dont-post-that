# Maybe Don't Post That

A skill for keeping leaders from torching their own reputation in public - and for catching the specific failure mode where a draft *feels* bold and motivating to the author but reads as contemptuous to everyone else.

The job here is **not** to sand every post into inoffensive corporate paste. Strong, opinionated, even provocative leadership writing is good. The job is to separate *provocative because it says something true and hard* from *provocative because it punches down and the author can't tell the difference.* The second kind always ends in a clarification post.

## The one mental model that matters

**Words travel without their author.** Once something is published, the context stays behind: your tone, your intent, the room you imagined, what you *meant*. Only the words travel.

And they have to survive on their own - in front of people who don't know you, don't share your assumptions, and won't fill in the gaps in your favor. A screenshot is simply the purest form of this: the words and nothing else, no thread above them, no face, no intent. The responsibility for what they carry is the author's, not the reader's.

A founder writes a recruiting post imagining an audience of ambitious 25-year-olds who'll be thrilled by the intensity. The actual audience also includes current employees (some of whom just had a baby), candidates the company is trying to win, competitors, journalists, and customers - none of whom share the author's context, and most of whom will read quickly and literally. The author writes for the imagined reader; the words are received by the real ones.

So the core test for any draft is: **does it hold up with nothing but the words - stripped of your intent, your context, your tone?** If what's left still says what you want it to say, ship it. If defending it would take "but what I meant was…" - that meaning never made it onto the page, and it isn't the reader's job to reconstruct it. Fix it now.

## Workflow when reviewing a draft

If the user references a post but hasn't pasted the actual text - "can you look at my LinkedIn draft?" - ask for the exact words first. You cannot pressure-test a paraphrase; the damage always lives in the specific phrasing, and that's exactly what gets quoted once the context is gone.

1. **Identify the real audience**, not the imagined one. Who will screenshot this? Who's the unintended reader who gets hurt or offended?
2. **Run the red-flag scan** (below). Name each one explicitly - don't soften.
3. **Find what the author actually wants to say.** There's almost always a legitimate, strong message buried under the tone-deaf framing. The intensity, the ambition, the high bar - those are usually fine. The *denigration* is the problem.
4. **Rewrite** to keep the spine and drop the contempt. Show the rewrite, then explain the specific swaps so the lesson transfers.
5. **State the residual risk.** No rewrite is risk-free. Tell them how the new version could still be read badly, so the decision to publish is theirs and informed.

**Default toward keeping the edge.** Do not dilute a sharp message unless the sharpness comes from contempt, exclusion, or avoidable collateral damage. A line that's merely blunt, demanding, or unflattering to the *competition* is usually fine - leave it. Only soften what hurts the wrong people or hands over a quotable line that turns on you once the context is gone.

## Output format

When reviewing a draft, ALWAYS respond using these five headings, in this order:

**Verdict** - one line: ship as-is / ship with edits / don't ship yet. Be decisive.
**Red flags** - each issue named, with the specific offending line quoted. If there are none, say so plainly and stop pretending to find problems.
**Stronger rewrite** - the full revised text, ready to paste. Not notes about what to change - the actual version.
**Why this works** - the specific swaps and what each one fixes, so the lesson transfers to the next post.
**Residual risk** - how the rewrite could still be read badly, so the publish decision is theirs and informed.

Skip the format only when the user isn't handing you a draft - e.g. they're asking a general question, or it's the already-published apology situation, where the apology-trap section governs instead.

## Red flags - scan every draft for these

**Denigration as motivation.** The draft builds up "us" by putting down a group: people who want balance, remote workers, parents, "9-to-5 people," a previous generation, the competition's employees. *Motivating by contempt is the single most common own-goal in leadership writing.* You can set an extremely high bar without insulting the people who don't clear it.

**"Don't apply if..." / exclusion-as-flex.** Listing things that disqualify someone ("if you want work-life balance, don't apply") feels like it filters for grit. It doesn't. It filters *out* the senior, high-leverage people who have options and boundaries, and filters *in* the desperate and the naïve. The people you most want are usually the ones most able to walk. Exclusionary bravado repels exactly the wrong half.

**Confusing intensity with self-sacrifice.** "Hard-working, resilient, fast" describes *output and standards* - legitimate. "Be ready to give up your evenings and weekends" describes *what you'll take from someone's life* - a liability disguised as a value. Sell the mission and the pace; never advertise the cost as if it were the perk.

**The mask-off line played as a joke.** A genuinely cutting line dressed up as humor. If it's challenged, the author will reach for "it was just a joke." That defense is a tell: see the apology section. If a line only works as a joke, and you're not sure it lands as one, it isn't one.

**Punching down.** Humor and edge are fine when aimed *up* (at yourself, at power, at your own company's absurdities) and dangerous when aimed *down* (at employees, candidates, junior people, anyone with less power than the author). Same joke, opposite outcome, depending on direction.

**Intent stated as if it were impact.** "I just meant..." Intent does not survive contact with an audience. Edit for how a line *lands*, not for what was *meant*. If the two diverge, the words are wrong - not the readers.

**The unforced "and here's who I despise."** Recruiting posts, culture posts, and manifestos don't need a villain. Adding one ("unlike the people who...") buys nothing and creates the screenshot.

## Saying the strong thing without the own-goal

The fix is almost never "be blander." It's "be honest about *your* offer instead of insulting people who'd decline it."

**Example - recruiting / hiring bar**
- ✗ "If you want a 9-to-5 with your weekends protected, do us both a favor and don't apply."
- ✓ "This is a high-intensity team. We ship fast and the work is genuinely demanding - that's the deal, and we're upfront about it so you can choose with eyes open."

The second version sets the *same* filter (it will absolutely deter balance-seekers) without the contempt. It repels by honest clarity, not by insult. Nobody screenshots it because there's no victim.

**Example - high standards**
- ✗ "We don't want 9-to-5 people who watch the clock."
- ✓ "We optimize for impact, not hours logged - which cuts both ways: we don't reward face-time, and we don't measure people by it."

**Example - urgency / a hard period**
- ✗ "If you can't handle pressure, this isn't for you."
- ✓ "The next two quarters are going to be intense and I want to be honest about that going in."

The pattern: **describe your reality and your offer; let people self-select.** Don't narrate the kind of person you're rejecting. The filter still works; the contempt is gone; there's no quotable insult.

## Calibration: drafts that should ship untouched

The skill earns its keep by *not* crying wolf. A draft can be blunt, demanding, opinionated, and unflattering to the competition and still be exactly right. When that's the case, say "ship it" and stop. Manufacturing a red flag to look useful is the fastest way to get this skill - and your counsel - ignored.

**Example - bold and fine:**
"We're a small team moving fast. The work is hard and the bar is high. If that energizes you, let's talk." → Ship it. It's intense and self-selecting, but it describes the offer instead of insulting anyone who'd pass.

**Example - edge aimed at the right target:**
"Our competitors ship a new pricing page every quarter and call it innovation. We'd rather ship something you'll actually use." → Ship it. The edge points up and sideways - at a rival's behavior - not down at employees or candidates. No victim, no screenshot.

The test is never "could *anyone* conceivably take offense?" - someone always can. The test is: **does the sharpness come from contempt, exclusion, or collateral damage to people with less power than the author?** If not, leave the edge alone and say so plainly.

## The AI-era angle (use when relevant)

A specific 2020s trap: leaders pairing "we want AI-native people" with "and we expect brutal hours." It reads as confused, because **leverage and grind point in opposite directions.** The entire promise of AI tooling is more output per person. Bragging about long hours *alongside* an AI-native pitch signals you haven't actually found the leverage - that the org still runs on burning people rather than on systems and tools. The sharper, more credible flex in this era is the opposite: "small team, serious tools, outsized output - we'd rather pay for leverage than for overtime." If a draft leans on grind as the headline value, flag the tension.

Automation is not a license to extract more. If a post implies "we automated X, so now you can do the work of three people," that's the quiet part out loud, and someone will quote it.

## Layoff and restructuring announcements

The highest-stakes post a leader writes, and the one where the gap between *how it feels to write* and *how it reads to the people losing their jobs* is widest. The author is usually drained, defensive, and genuinely sad - and reaches, in good faith, for words that protect themselves. That instinct is the whole problem. Scan hard for these:

**Centering the leader's pain.** "This is the hardest thing I've ever had to do." Maybe true, but the person reading it just lost their income and health insurance - your difficulty is not the headline. Grief is fine in one honest line; a paragraph about *your* anguish reads as asking the laid-off to comfort you.

**Euphemism.** "Rightsizing," "streamlining," "letting go," "transitioning," "saying goodbye to." Say *laying off* or *cutting jobs.* The people affected know exactly what happened; dressing it up insults them and signals you can't face it.

**The optimism pivot in the same breath.** "...and we're more excited than ever about the future." Celebrating the company's prospects in the same post where you cut people reads as: *they were the dead weight holding us back.* The future-hype belongs in a different message, on a different day.

**Numbers as anesthetic.** "Reducing headcount by 15%." "Impacting 200 roles." Roles don't have families; people do. Name that these are people, not a percentage.

**Blame laundering.** "The market forced our hand." "These were incredibly difficult decisions." Passive constructions that make the cuts sound like weather. You made the decision. Own it in the active voice.

**The thank-you that reads as disposal.** "I'm so grateful for everything they gave us." Gratitude with no concrete support behind it is a kiss-off. If you're thanking people, the post should also say what you're actually doing for them - severance, healthcare runway, references, hiring-network help.

**Posting it for the personal-brand engagement.** A layoff announcement that doubles as a LinkedIn thought-leadership moment, complete with lessons-learned takeaways, is grotesque to anyone in the affected group. If the post is really *about* the leader looking thoughtful, don't post it.

**The AI-era version:** "AI has made us more efficient, so we're reducing the team." Even if true, this is the quiet part screamed into a microphone - it tells everyone remaining that they're next, and hands every journalist the story. If automation genuinely drove the cut, the humane framing is about what you're doing for the people displaced, not about the productivity win.

**What good looks like:**
- ✗ "Today is a gut-wrenching day. After much soul-searching, we've made the difficult decision to say goodbye to some incredible teammates as we rightsize for the exciting road ahead."
- ✓ "Today we're laying off about 60 people - roughly 12% of the company. This is the result of decisions I made, not anyone's performance. Here's exactly what we're providing: [severance, X months of healthcare, accelerated equity, a referral list we're personally working]. To the people leaving: I'm sorry, and I mean it."

The good version is shorter, names the number, owns the decision, leads with what the affected people get, and saves the company's future for another day. It doesn't perform anguish and it doesn't ask for sympathy. That's the bar.

A note on sequencing: the people being laid off should *never* learn it from a public post. Direct, private notification comes first - the public statement is for everyone else, and writing it as if the affected are the audience (they're not, by the time it's posted) is a category error worth flagging if the draft is structured that way.

## When it's already out: the apology trap

Most "clarification" posts make it worse and start a second news cycle. Before drafting one, diagnose honestly which situation this is:

**The post was actually fine and got willfully misread.** Rare, but real. Here: hold the line briefly and calmly, or stay silent. Do not grovel for something defensible.

**The post was genuinely bad.** Then a *real* correction is short, owns the substance, changes the actual thing, and does not re-argue. "I got this wrong. Here's what we're changing." Full stop.

**Avoid the non-apology**, which is what leaders reflexively reach for and which reliably pours fuel on the fire. Its tells:
- *"It was just a joke - people took it too seriously."* Explaining the joke tells your audience the failure was *their* comprehension, not your words. It's an insult on top of an insult.
- *"What I was really trying to say is..."* Reframing the message people clearly received as the message you wish you'd sent. Reads as gaslighting.
- *"Anyone who knows me knows that's not who I am."* / *"Some of my closest colleagues are exactly the people that post seemed to dismiss."* Producing character witnesses instead of addressing the words. Nobody accused you of being a bad person; they reacted to what you published.
- *"Sorry if it came across the wrong way."* Passive voice that locates the problem in how it was received, not in what was said.

**The best apology post is the one you never have to write** - which is the entire point of catching it at the draft stage. If a leader is already reaching for "let me explain what I really meant," the honest counsel is usually: a one-line genuine correction, or nothing, but not a paragraph re-litigating intent.

## Tone for delivering this to the user

These are often high-status, time-pressured people who think the draft is great. Be direct and specific - vague hedging wastes their time and they'll ignore it. But aim the critique at the *words and how they'll land*, never at the person ("this line will read as contemptuous," not "you're being contemptuous"). Always pair a flag with a rewrite - pointing out the problem without offering the better version is half a job. And when a draft is genuinely good and bold and *should* ship as-is, say so plainly; the skill loses all credibility if it flinches at every strong sentence.
